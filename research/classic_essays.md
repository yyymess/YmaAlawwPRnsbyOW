## 1. "Reflecting on 18 years at Google" — Ian Hickson (Hixie) [V]
- **Company:** Google
- **Date:** 2023-11-22
- **URL:** https://ln.hixie.ch/?start=1700627373&count=1
- **Argument:** The HTML5 spec editor and Flutter co-founder describes early post-IPO Google as genuinely good people steered by "don't be evil," with executives giving frank weekly answers and Eric Schmidt walking the whole company through board discussions. Over time silos crept in (Google+ locked buildings), then the culture eroded: decisions went from benefiting users, to benefiting Google, to benefiting whoever made the decision. The 2023 layoffs, driven by quarter-to-quarter stock price, destroyed trust: people stopped taking risks and started hoarding knowledge to be irreplaceable. He blames Sundar Pichai's lack of vision and "inept middle management," and says power must move "from the CFO's office" back to someone with long-term vision.
- **Memorable detail:** He used to eagerly attend every all-hands; by the end he could "predict the answers executives would give word for word." Also: the core Flutter design doc was a single whiteboard photo. Also: "If you talk to therapists in the bay area... all their Google clients are unhappy."
- **Short quote:** "Decisions went from being made for the benefit of users, to the benefit of Google, to the benefit of whoever was making the decision." (long; short form: "Transparency evaporated.")
- **Visual:** A three-step staircase of arrows pointing at "Users" -> "Google" -> "Me"; or a TGIF microphone where the exec's speech bubble is pre-filled before the question is asked.

## 2. "How Microsoft Lost the API War" — Joel Spolsky [V]
- **Company:** Microsoft
- **Date:** 2004-06-13
- **URL:** https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2004/06/13/how-microsoft-lost-the-api-war/
- **Argument:** Joel splits Microsoft into two tribes: the "Raymond Chen Camp" (heroic backward compatibility: old programs must keep working) and the "MSDN Magazine Camp" (keep churning out new gigantic technologies — COM+, .NET, Avalon, WinFS — to give people a reason to buy upgrades). The MSDN camp won. He argues Microsoft "got big enough, with too many developers, and they were too addicted to upgrade revenues," so it broke compatibility and lost developers to the web. It is less about MBAs than about revenue-driven churn replacing craftsman's care for the customer.
- **Memorable detail:** Windows engineers found that SimCity used memory after freeing it, so they added special code to Windows that detected SimCity and ran the memory allocator in a special mode just so the game kept working. Also: Ballmer jumping around the stage shouting "Developers, developers, developers."
- **Short quote:** "Inside Microsoft, the MSDN Magazine Camp has won the battle."
- **Visual:** A tug-of-war rope between a lone engineer holding a 1983 floppy disk and a crowd holding a giant stack of glossy magazines labeled with acronyms; or a Windows box with a tiny SimCity skyline being propped up by a hand-drawn crutch.

## 3. "Why I left Google" — James Whittaker [V via mirror]
- **Company:** Google (author left for Microsoft)
- **Date:** 2012-03-13 (originally on his MSDN blog "JW on Tech")
- **URL:** original https://blogs.msdn.microsoft.com/jw_on_tech/2012/03/13/why-i-left-google/ (dead; MS archive moved); full-text mirror https://pastebin.com/uQyTCFeq
- **Argument:** Under Eric Schmidt, Google ran "like an innovation factory" — founder's awards, peer bonuses, 20% time, Google Labs — with ad revenue quietly paying for it. When Facebook threatened the ads business, Larry Page made social "state-owned": Google+ became a corporate mandate and every product had to orbit it. The trappings of entrepreneurship were dismantled ("more wood behind fewer arrows"), and the "new Google" knew what the future should be rather than letting employees invent it.
- **Memorable details:** "Suddenly, 20% meant half-assed." Google Labs shut down, App Engine fees raised, free APIs deprecated. His teenage daughter, after a Google+ demo: social isn't a product, "social is people and the people are on Facebook." Google as "the rich kid" who built his own party after not being invited — and no one came.
- **Short quote:** "The Google I left was an advertising company with a single corporate-mandated focus."
- **Visual:** A Google Labs beaker with a "CLOSED" sign hanging on it; or a rich kid alone at a party with a "+" balloon and empty chairs.

## 4. "Why I left Google to join Grab" — Steve Yegge [V via mirror; Medium blocked]
- **Company:** Google (author previously 7 years at Amazon)
- **Date:** 2018-01 (Medium; widely reported ~2018-01-24/25)
- **URL:** https://steve-yegge.medium.com/why-i-left-google-to-join-grab-86dfffc0be84 (mirror: https://startupanz.com/left-google-join-grab/)
- **Argument:** After ~13 years, Yegge says Google "can no longer innovate" for four reasons: it is conservative (gatekeeping and risk aversion are the norm), mired in politics, arrogant as a collective "we," and "100% competitor-focused rather than customer focused." He lists the me-too launches (Google+ vs Facebook, Allo vs WhatsApp, Home vs Echo, Assistant vs Siri) and the turned-down beloved services (Reader, Hangouts). His foil is Grab's "go to the ground" mantra: Google employees "basically never interact with their customers."
- **Memorable detail:** The ivory tower vs the motorbike: on his first week at Grab he rode pillion on a motorbike taxi into oncoming traffic in Jakarta and thought "I'm gonna die" — while Google teams back home were "bickering with each other about what to launch."
- **Short quote:** "They simply don't have innovation in their DNA any more."
- **Visual:** An ivory tower with a telescope pointed sideways at a competitor's building instead of down at the people on the street.

## 5. Steve Jobs, "The Lost Interview" (Triumph of the Nerds, with Robert X. Cringely) [V transcript]
- **Company:** Xerox, IBM, Apple-under-Sculley (Jobs was running NeXT at the time)
- **Date:** recorded 1995; rediscovered and released as a film in Nov 2011
- **URL:** transcript https://sameerbajaj.com/jobs/ ; context https://www.npr.org/sections/alltechconsidered/2011/11/16/142373973/steve-jobs-dishes-on-the-tech-business-in-lost-interview-from-1995
- **Argument:** The canonical statement of the genre. In a company with a monopoly, building a better copier or computer doesn't make the company more successful, so the sales and marketing people get promoted, "and they end up running the companies," while "the product people get driven out of the decision-making forums." The product genius that won the monopoly "gets rotted out." Second idea: as companies grow they try to institutionalize the process behind their success, and "people get very confused that the process is the content" — IBM "has the best process people in the world. They just forgot about the content." Also: Sculley came from PepsiCo, where "a new product was, like, a new-size bottle."
- **Memorable detail:** PARC researchers called Xerox's executives "toner heads" — copier people who toured the Alto, the GUI, Ethernet and email, "had no clue about what they were seeing," and "grabbed defeat from the greatest victory in the computer industry." Plus Jobs on Sculley: he got on a rocket ship about to leave the pad, then "thought that he built the rocket ship."
- **Short quote:** "The product people get driven out of the decision-making forums."
- **Visual:** Suited "toner heads" in a gallery staring blankly at a glowing Alto screen, holding a toner cartridge; or a Pepsi bottle labeled "NEW!" that is just a slightly bigger bottle.

## 6. "The Long-Forgotten Flight That Sent Boeing Off Course" — Jerry Useem, The Atlantic [V via mirror]
- **Company:** Boeing (and McDonnell Douglas)
- **Date:** 2019-11-20 (The Atlantic, Ideas)
- **URL:** https://www.theatlantic.com/ideas/archive/2019/11/how-boeing-lost-its-bearings/602188/ (paywalled; mirror https://dewaynenet.wordpress.com/2019/12/06/the-long-forgotten-flight-that-sent-boeing-off-course/)
- **Argument:** "For about 80 years, Boeing basically functioned as an association of engineers": executives held patents and designed wings; as late as the mid-'90s the CFO barely talked to Wall Street. The 1997 "reverse takeover" of McDonnell Douglas put McDonnell's cost-cutting, stock-watching culture in charge ("McDonnell Douglas bought Boeing with Boeing's money"). In 2001 CEO Phil Condit and President Harry Stonecipher moved HQ to Chicago, deliberately 1,700 miles from the nearest airliner assembly line, so the corporate center wouldn't be "drawn into day-to-day business operations." Useem traces the 737 MAX disasters back to that divorce.
- **Memorable detail:** The HQ-reveal PR flight took off from Seattle with three flight plans — Denver, Dallas, Chicago — and nobody aboard knew which one. Also: in Condit's office in 2000, 54 white roses celebrating the day's closing stock price. New slogans: "A passion for affordability," "Less family, more team."
- **Short quote:** "A company once driven by engineers became driven by finance."
- **Visual:** A 737 on a runway with three dotted flight paths fanning out to three city skylines; or a vase of white roses next to a stock ticker on an executive desk.

## 7. Mini-Microsoft blog ("Who da'Punk") + the Microsoft towel saga [S + secondary sources V]
- **Company:** Microsoft
- **Date:** blog launched 2004-07-06 ("Blast off for Mini-Microsoft"); peak 2005–2006; last posts 2012–2014. Towels cut 2004, restored 2006-05-18.
- **URLs:** http://minimsft.blogspot.com/ (blocked from here); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mini-Microsoft ; towel saga: https://customerthink.com/throwing-in-the-towels-at-microsoft/ , https://www.engadget.com/2006-05-23-come-work-at-microsoft-you-get-free-towels.html ; Mini's reaction post http://minimsft.blogspot.com/2006/05/microsofts-may-18th-2006-big-turning.html
- **Argument:** An anonymous Microsoft insider argued the company had grown fat and should be cut down to a leaner "Mini-Microsoft"; the comment threads became a "soap box for employee discontent" (FT) about stack-ranked reviews, executive pay, a flat stock price and middle-management bloat. BusinessWeek dubbed the author "Microsoft's Deep Throat" (Sept 2005). It pre-figured Blind (which marketed itself as "Mini-Microsoft 2.0").
- **Memorable detail (Microsoft's "soda moment"):** In 2004 cost cuts, Microsoft stopped laundering free towels for employees who showered after cycling to work — reportedly at the behest of an HR exec recruited from Dell. "Towels" became the shorthand for leadership that didn't get it; at a May 18, 2006 town hall HR chief Lisa Brummel announced the towels were coming back (myMicrosoft). Widely quoted line: "It's not the towels, it's what the towels represent." (Also: in the early '90s many Microsoft PCs ran a little cartoon face that smiled or frowned with the stock price — per Eichenwald.)
- **Short quote:** "The towels became the symbol of poor leadership."
- **Visual:** An empty towel rack in a locker room with a small printed memo taped to it; two years later, a single folded towel returning on a silver tray.

## 8. "Microsoft's Lost Decade" — Kurt Eichenwald, Vanity Fair [V via PDF copy]
- **Company:** Microsoft (Ballmer era, ~2000–2012)
- **Date:** August 2012 issue (online July 2012)
- **URL:** https://www.vanityfair.com/news/business/2012/08/microsoft-lost-mojo-steve-ballmer (PDF copy read: https://athena.ecs.csus.edu/~buckley/CSc191/Vanity_Fair_Microsofts_Lost_Decade_Aug_2012.pdf)
- **Argument:** A "lean competition machine led by young visionaries" mutated into "something bloated and bureaucracy-laden" that rewarded managers who strangled threatening ideas. Once the stock stopped minting millionaires, people "turned into people trying to move up the ladder"; more managers meant more meetings, more memos. Stack ranking (forced bell curve) was cited by every interviewee as the most destructive process: stars avoided working with other stars, and staff worked to make sure colleagues failed. An e-book device was killed in the late '90s because it didn't run Windows/Office ("Windows was the god"). Closes with Jobs (via Isaacson) blaming salesmen taking over under Ballmer.
- **Memorable details:** The forced curve — on a team of 10, "two people were going to get a great review, seven... mediocre... and one... terrible," no matter what. Market cap flip: Microsoft $510B (Dec 2000) vs Apple $4.8B; by mid-2012 Apple $541B, Microsoft $249B. "One Apple product... has higher sales than everything Microsoft has to offer." Ballmer laughing at the iPhone in 2007.
- **Short quote:** "No matter how good everyone was... one was going to get a terrible review."
- **Visual:** A bell curve drawn on a whiteboard with ten stick-figure engineers lined up underneath, one pushed off the right edge; or Manu Cornet's famous 2011 org-chart cartoon in which Microsoft's divisions point guns at each other [from memory, not re-verified].

## 9. "Microsoft's Creative Destruction" — Dick Brass (former Microsoft VP), NYT op-ed [V via Seattle Times reprint]
- **Company:** Microsoft
- **Date:** 2010-02-04 (New York Times)
- **URL:** https://www.nytimes.com/2010/02/04/opinion/04brass.html (reprint read: https://www.seattletimes.com/business/microsoft/commentary-microsofts-creative-destruction/)
- **Argument:** Written the week the iPad was unveiled: Microsoft keeps reporting record earnings — almost entirely from Windows and Office "first developed decades ago" — yet "no longer brings us the future" (tablets, e-books, smartphones, search, music, social). It "never developed a true system for innovation," and colleagues argue it built "a system to thwart innovation": entrenched divisions protect their turf and kill internal upstarts. He compares Microsoft to General Motors living off trucks and SUVs.
- **Memorable details:** ClearType (sharper on-screen text) was attacked by threatened groups: Windows engineers falsely claimed it made displays go haywire, the head of Office said it was fuzzy and gave him headaches, and a VP offered support only if the programmers were transferred to him — it took a decade to ship fully. The 2001 Tablet PC was sabotaged by an Office VP who preferred keyboards and refused to adapt Office for the pen.
- **Short quote:** "It actually developed a system to thwart innovation."
- **Visual:** A stylus being blocked at a castle drawbridge labeled "OFFICE"; or a crisp letter "a" being smudged by several hands reaching in from offscreen.

## 10. Cory Doctorow — "Tiktok's enshittification" (2023) and "The moral injury of having your work enshittified" (2023) [V]
- **Company:** Platforms generally — Amazon, Facebook, Google, Twitter, TikTok
- **Dates:** 2023-01-21 (Pluralistic; Wired version "The 'Enshittification' of TikTok" 2023-01-23); follow-up 2023-11-25
- **URLs:** https://pluralistic.net/2023/01/21/potemkin-ai/ ; https://www.wired.com/story/tiktok-platforms-cory-doctorow/ ; https://pluralistic.net/2023/11/25/moral-injury/
- **Argument:** "Here is how platforms die": first they are good to users; then they abuse users to benefit business customers; then they abuse business customers "to claw back all the value for themselves. Then, they die." (Amazon search, Facebook's pivot-to-video, Twitter's monetized feeds.) The November follow-up is the genre piece for engineers: tech workers' perks (massages, kombucha, gourmet cafeterias, egg-freezing) and their "workplace democracy" at town halls came not from respect but from the discipline of a tight labor market; vocational awe ("don't be evil," "make a dent in the universe") kept them working 16-hour days. Once monopoly removed market discipline and layoffs removed labor scarcity, "techies started losing the argument" — and with it their power to defend users. He explicitly responds to Hixie: it wasn't a change in leaders, it was a change in discipline.
- **Memorable details:** Search Amazon for "cat beds": the first screen is all ads; first five screens are 50% ads. Google laid off 12,000 "just months after the company does a stock buyback that would cover all those salaries for the next 27 years." Blue badges vs green badges (contractors) using different entrances, staggered so they wouldn't queue together.
- **Short quote:** "First, they are good to their users... Then, they die." / "Money talks, bullshit walks."
- **Visual:** A three-stage platform life cycle drawn as a vending machine: free candy -> candy behind ads -> machine eating the coins and dispensing nothing; or blue-badge and green-badge stick figures entering by separate doors.

## 11. "The end of 0% interest rates" series — Gergely Orosz, The Pragmatic Engineer [V, partly paywalled]
- **Company:** Big Tech and VC-funded startups generally (Orosz ex-Uber, ex-Skype/Microsoft)
- **Dates:** Part 1 (startups/industry) 2024-01-09; Part 2 (software engineers) 2024-01-30; Parts 3–4 (managers; engineering practices) follow
- **URLs:** https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/zirp ; https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/zirp-software-engineers
- **Argument:** The engineer-friendly 2010s were a "golden age" because the longest-ever stretch of near-zero interest rates (2008–2022) coincided with two cheap growth engines, smartphones and cloud. With ZIRP over: pressure for profitability, fewer VC dollars, Big Tech gets bigger, layoffs peak (late 2022/early 2023), applications triple, offers are harder to negotiate, "performance expectations are going up," fewer engineering-manager roles, and even engineering fashions shift (monoliths over microservices). Not a lament so much as an engineer-practical account of why the pampering ended — the macro explanation sitting under every "free soda" story.
- **Memorable details:** At Uber, hiring managers used to blow past headcount to close a standout candidate and "trade" headcount from next half's budget — now gone. Voluntary attrition expected to fall from ~10–15% to ~5–10%. A recurring Orosz meme: "1. Go 2. Microservices 3. ??? 4. Profit."
- **Short quote:** "Performance expectations are going up."
- **Visual:** A line chart of interest rates lying flat on the floor for a decade, with a party (balloons, kombucha taps) sitting on top of it, then the line shooting up and popping the balloons.

## 12. "When the Mismanagerial Class Destroys Great Companies" — Marko Jukic, Palladium [V]
- **Company:** Intel, Boeing, Sony (also GE, Raytheon/RTX)
- **Date:** 2024-08-30
- **URL:** https://www.palladiummag.com/2024/08/30/when-the-mismanagerial-class-destroys-great-companies/
- **Argument:** In 2005 three iconic engineering companies got leaders who weren't founding-engineer types: Intel's Paul Otellini (its first CEO without an engineering background), Boeing's James McNerney (first with no aerospace experience), and Sony's Nobuyuki Idei era wrapping up. Jukic argues it's not simply "blame the MBAs": the problem is a theory of the company as a vehicle for capturing profit (or for intangible "stakeholder" goals) rather than for creating value through better products. When the line of founders and founding engineers runs out, the only socially acceptable outsider executives are profit-capturers, and great firms get "managed into irrelevance," then turned into portfolio holding companies.
- **Memorable detail:** Otellini, shortly after taking over, declined Steve Jobs's request to build the chip for the secret iPhone project because the costs looked too high and the volume too low — saving money in the short run and giving up leadership of computing's next era. Stats: since 2021 Intel revenue down by a third; since 2018 Boeing down a quarter.
- **Short quote:** "Companies being enthusiastically managed into irrelevance."
- **Visual:** A relay race where the baton passes from a lab-coated engineer to a suited runner who runs the wrong way toward a giant dollar sign; or Otellini's desk with a tiny unmarked phone prototype pushed back across it.

## 13. "Inside Intel, employees say the famous culture gradually fell apart" — Lila MacLellan, Fortune [V via mailing-list copy]
- **Company:** Intel
- **Date:** 2025-10-01
- **URL:** https://fortune.com/2025/10/01/intel-company-culture-changes-grove-tan-nvidia/ (text copy: https://mailman.anu.edu.au/pipermail/link/2025-October/042415.html)
- **Argument:** Oral-history style: Andy Grove's Intel ("Only the paranoid survive," flat structure, "constructive confrontation," engineers rewarded for hitting production targets) survived Craig Barrett, but Paul Otellini, "the company's first non-engineer CEO," began changes "the company never recovered from": experimentation lost its shine, awards were minimized. CFO-turned-CEO Bob Swan (2019–21) was a penny-pincher who wiped away even small perks. Engineer Pat Gelsinger's 2021 return briefly revived Grove's spirit; then mass layoffs (15,000 in 2024, ~25,000 more under Lip-Bu Tan in 2025) and an "invisible," investor-facing CEO.
- **Memorable details:** Grove-era rewards: silicon wafers signed by Andy Grove; free ball-game tickets for your family. By the end, a thank-you gift card was "really hard to get approved" ($25–$100). Layoffs got euphemistic program names like "Corporate People Movement," announced at "Business Unit Meetings" — BUMs. The new CEO was spotted in person once, at an ice-cream social meant to soften a return-to-office mandate.
- **Short quote:** "I haven't seen him, even by accident."
- **Visual:** A silicon wafer with a signature scrawled on it, framed on a cubicle wall, beside a $25 gift card sitting in an "approval pending" inbox tray.

## 14. "Fork Yeah! The Rise and Development of illumos" — Bryan Cantrill, USENIX LISA talk [V slides + secondary write-up]
- **Company:** Sun Microsystems -> Oracle
- **Date:** 2011-12 (LISA '11, Boston)
- **URLs:** slides https://www.usenix.org/legacy/events/lisa11/tech/slides/cantrill.pdf ; video https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-zRN7XLCRhc ; write-up https://www.startupgrind.com/blog/former-sun-engineer-says-oracle-only-about-one-thing-money/
- **Argument:** A "people's history" of Solaris in which quality rises and falls with who's in charge: when "management took over day-to-day operations" of Solaris 2.4, quality slipped; for 2.5 "the engineers 'took over'" with Jeff Bonwick as gatekeeper and authority to "rip it out if it's broken," and engineers "would never again relinquish control." Sun's own epitaph (Scott McNealy): "Kicked butt, had fun, didn't cheat, loved our customers, changed computing forever." After Oracle closed the deal (Feb 2010) it became clear that eulogy was "the antithesis of Oracle": OpenSolaris source updates silently stopped (Aug 18, 2010, after an internal memo of Aug 13), and the engineers left en masse to found illumos.
- **Memorable details:** Within 90 days of the memo, the entire DTrace team, all primary inventors of ZFS, and the primary zones and networking engineers had left Oracle. The live talk's most-quoted riff: don't anthropomorphize Larry Ellison — it's like a lawnmower: you stick your hand in, it chops it off; the lawnmower doesn't hate you, it "can't hate you."
- **Short quote:** "Don't anthropomorphize the lawnmower."
- **Visual:** A riding lawnmower rolling across a lawn shaped like the Sun logo, while small engineer figures carry boxes labeled DTrace/ZFS out the side gate.

## 15. "What Happened to Yahoo" — Paul Graham [V]  (+ companion: Brad Garlinghouse's "Peanut Butter Manifesto," 2006 [V via mirror])
- **Company:** Yahoo
- **Dates:** PG essay August 2010; Garlinghouse internal memo Oct/Nov 2006 (leaked to WSJ, published 2006-11-18)
- **URLs:** https://paulgraham.com/yahoo.html ; memo text: https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/memo-the-internal-peanut-butter-memo-from-yahoo-is-still-relevant/
- **Argument (PG):** Yahoo had two problems Google didn't: easy money and "ambivalence about being a technology company." It insisted on calling itself a "media company": project managers were "producers," divisions were "properties," and "Yahoo treated programming as a commodity" — product managers and designers decided, programmers just translated specs into code. That lowered the hiring bar and started a "death spiral" because good programmers want to work with good programmers. "Most technology companies eventually get taken over by suits and middle managers. At Yahoo it felt as if they'd deliberately accelerated this process." The euphemism used to kill hacker culture: "adult supervision."
- **Argument (Garlinghouse, an SVP):** Yahoo spread "a thin layer of investment" across everything like peanut butter; massive redundancy, silos, no ownership ("Flickr vs. Photos"), people "phoning it in," weak performers rewarded; "heads must roll."
- **Memorable details:** PG realized in his cubicle that Yahoo's revenue was a de facto Ponzi scheme (VC-funded startups buying Yahoo banner ads) and jumped up like Archimedes shouting not "Eureka!" but "Sell!" He told David Filo Yahoo should buy Google because the programmers all used it; Filo said search was only 6% of traffic. Sales chief Anil Singh's team flew to Procter & Gamble and came back with million-dollar banner orders. Garlinghouse's line: "I hate peanut butter. We all should."
- **Short quote:** "They didn't want to be a bunch of hackers. They wanted to be suits."
- **Visual:** A slice of toast enormous as a map, with a too-thin smear of peanut butter labeled with dozens of product names; or a programmer at a desk receiving a spec through a mail slot from a "producer" in a director's chair.

## 16. "Car Guys vs. Bean Counters: The Battle for the Soul of American Business" — Bob Lutz (book) [S: reviews + interviews read]
- **Company:** General Motors (the non-tech archetype tech people cite)
- **Date:** June 2011 (book); Lutz's Aztek post-mortem in Road & Track, Oct 2014
- **URLs:** https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/11276949-car-guys-vs-bean-counters ; review https://www.thetruthaboutcars.com/2011/06/book-review-car-guys-vs-bean-counters-the-battle-for-the-soul-of-american-business/ ; Aztek: https://gmauthority.com/blog/2014/10/beautiful-failure-bob-lutz-talks-pontiac-aztek/
- **Argument:** GM's postwar golden age was run by "true car guys" like designers Harley Earl and Bill Mitchell. Starting around 1977, when a corporate functionary replaced Mitchell and product creation moved under a finance-dominated planning function, "the Empire of finance, accounting, law and order... struck back": design became "a link in the chain," and the "volcano of creation" became a quiet mountain "spewing forth mediocrity upon mediocrity" (reviewer's paraphrase of Lutz). Product decisions were made by spreadsheets, cost targets and internal metrics instead of people who loved cars; Lutz (GM vice chairman 2001–2010) recounts fighting that culture.
- **Memorable detail — the Pontiac Aztek:** Chairman Rick Wagoner decreed 40% of new products must be "innovative," which "started a trend toward setting internal goals that meant nothing to the customer." The Aztek came last in product clinics (focus-group verdict: "I wouldn't take it as a gift"), the program chief banned negative comments, and afterwards a Pontiac executive said he was proud: it "made all our internal goals, we made the timing." Also: the "hider" trick of rounding off adjacent plastic interior pieces so customers wouldn't notice misalignment.
- **Short quote:** "Setting internal goals that meant nothing to the customer."
- **Visual:** The boxy Pontiac Aztek on a stage, with a clipboard beside it showing every checkbox ticked — and an audience of focus-group stick figures holding their noses.

## 17. "The Era of the Business Idiot" — Ed Zitron, Where's Your Ed At [S: summary/quote index read; original host blocked here]
- **Company:** Big Tech generally (Google, Microsoft, Meta, etc.) — the "manager class" thesis, distinct from Zitron's Google essays already covered
- **Date:** 2025-05-21 (plus a 3-part "Better Offline" podcast series, May–June 2025)
- **URL:** https://www.wheresyoured.at/the-era-of-the-business-idiot/ (quote index: https://edzitron.spicytakes.org/post/2025-05-21-the-era-of-the-business-idiot)
- **Argument:** Modern companies are run by "Business Idiots": executives and managers who don't use, understand or care about their products, customers or workers, yet hold all the power. Zitron traces it to Milton Friedman's shareholder-primacy doctrine (and the Welch-era MBA playbook) that redefined a good business as one that grows its stock rather than one that makes good things. Management stops meaning doing work and becomes a culture of dominance and value extraction; generative AI is the Business Idiot's dream tool because it produces an impression of work, which is what such managers already do.
- **Memorable images:** "The symbolic executive." The higher you go, "the further you are from problems or purpose." An AI summary as exactly the kind of generic précis "a know-nothing executive or manager would" write of your work.
- **Short quote:** "The higher up you go at a company, the further you are from problems or purpose."
- **Visual:** An executive at the top of a tall ladder in the clouds, holding a telescope pointed the wrong way, while the actual product (a small machine) sits at the ladder's foot; or a robot and a suit shaking hands over a stack of blank reports.

## 18. "Jony Ive's Fraying Relationship With Apple" — Tripp Mickle, Wall Street Journal [S: secondary coverage read; WSJ paywalled; Apple disputed it]
- **Company:** Apple (Tim Cook era)

## 24. "The Gervais Principle, Or The Office According to 'The Office'" — Venkatesh Rao, Ribbonfarm [V]

## Recurring archetypes (patterns across these stories)

1. **The perk-withdrawal moment (the "free soda" signal).** A tiny, visible perk is clawed back with a memo and the engineers read it as the real announcement: Blank's 50-cent soda; Microsoft's towels (2004) and the "don't bring sodas home" fridge sign (Su); Intel's $25 gift card that's "hard to get approved"; the dot-com espresso machine wheeled out on a hand cart a week before layoffs; Google's stapler-and-microkitchen "durable savings." The perk was never the point; the message is that someone's full-time job is now watching pennies. *Drawable: a sign taped to a fridge.*

2. **The Pepsi executive / the outsider with no "content."** Leadership passes from builders to people who measure: Sculley from PepsiCo ("a new product was a new-size bottle"), Xerox's "toner heads," Otellini (Intel's first non-engineer CEO) passing on the iPhone, McDonnell Douglas executives running Boeing, Fiorina at HP, CFO Bob Swan at Intel, Hickson's wish to move power "from the CFO's office." Jobs's formula: in a monopoly, the people who can move the numbers are sales and marketing, "and they end up running the companies." *Drawable: a baton handed from a lab coat to a suit.*

3. **Moving away from the factory floor.** Leaders put physical or psychological distance between themselves and the people who build: Boeing's HQ flight to Chicago, 1,700 miles from the assembly line; Yegge's "ivory tower" where Google employees "never interact with their customers"; Intel's "invisible" CEO seen once at an ice-cream social; Ive's design team reporting to Operations; Zitron's "the higher up you go... the further you are from problems or purpose." *Drawable: an airplane carrying the executive suite away from a factory.*

4. **The metric that eats the product (process becomes the thing).** A proxy replaces the goal and everyone optimizes it: IBM's $20 EPS "Death March 2015"; GM's Aztek that "made all our internal goals"; Microsoft's stack-ranked bell curve; Meta's token-count leaderboard; Bezos's "process as proxy"; Google+ "higher review scores"; Jobs's "the process is the content." *Drawable: a checklist with every box ticked next to a broken product.*

5. **Founders' rhetoric turned into marketing for its own replacement.** The old culture survives only as iconography: HP's "Back to the Garage" ads during the Compaq fight; Google's "don't be evil" and 2004 IPO letter quoted back at it; Facebook's Little Red Book posters vs. keystroke logging; Amazon's "Day 1" building nameplate vs. the "bureaucracy mailbox"; Sun's "Kicked butt, had fun... changed computing forever" eulogy. *Drawable: a framed motto on a wall with the paint peeling.*

6. **Turf, fiefdoms, and internal knife-fights.** Growth breeds silos that kill internal upstarts: Microsoft groups sabotaging ClearType and the Tablet PC (Brass), Windows/Office as "the god" (Eichenwald), Yahoo's "Flickr vs. Photos" redundancy (Peanut Butter), Google+ locking buildings (Hickson), Manu Cornet's 2011 "Bonkers World" org-chart cartoon of Microsoft divisions pointing guns at each other [from memory, not re-verified]. *Drawable: castles with drawbridges up, each labeled with a product name.*

7. **The elves leave (talent exodus as the real obituary).** The best people go first and quietly: the entire DTrace and ZFS teams gone from Oracle within 90 days (Cantrill); Bell Labs down to four basic-research physicists; Hickson, Whittaker, Yegge, Su posting their exits; Rao's Gervais life cycle in which the Sociopaths and Losers exit and "the Clueless start to dominate"; PG's death spiral ("good programmers want to work with other good programmers"). *Drawable: small figures with backpacks walking single file out a side gate at dusk.*

8. **Layoffs as trust-breaker (and the euphemism machine).** Layoffs during record profits change behavior permanently: people stop taking risks and start hoarding knowledge (Hickson); "resources" and "resource actions" (IBM); "Corporate People Movement" announced at "BUMs" (Intel); a 10% cut announced a month in advance (Meta 2026); Google's 12,000 cut months after a buyback that could fund them for 27 years (Doctorow). *Drawable: a badge reader flashing red.*

9. **Scarcity, not respect (the macro explanation).** The engineer-friendly era was a product of cheap money and a tight labor market: Orosz's ZIRP golden age; Doctorow's "fear... mistaken for respect" (perks as discipline of competition for labor); Yahoo's banner-ad "Ponzi scheme" that hid the need to compete on search (PG). When money gets expensive or a monopoly is secured, the pampering ends. *Drawable: an interest-rate line lying flat under a party, then shooting up and popping the balloons.*

10. **"Adult supervision" and the reorg as cure.** The decline is often packaged as maturity: Yahoo's "adult supervision," Google's "more wood behind fewer arrows," Meta's "year of efficiency," Jassy's "world's largest startup," Garlinghouse's "heads must roll." The fix proposed is almost always another layer of process or another flattening, which tends to deepen the condition. *Drawable: a stern babysitter figure placing a playpen around a workbench.*
