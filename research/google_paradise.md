## 1. 2004 IPO Founders' Letter ("An Owner's Manual for Google's Shareholders")
- Year: 2004 (filed with the S-1, April 2004; IPO August 2004)
- Description: Larry Page and Sergey Brin's letter in the IPO prospectus, titled "An Owner's Manual" for shareholders. It opens with the line "Google is not a conventional company. We do not intend to become one." (Page & Brin, 2004). It also explained the dual-class share structure and pledged long-term focus over quarterly earnings.
- Source: https://abc.xyz/investor/founders-letters/2004-ipo-letter/ ; text mirror: https://www.thestreet.com/opinion/text-of-googles-letter-to-prospective-shareholders-10157519
- Visual: a thick stock prospectus with a hand-scribbled sticky note on the cover; a Wall Street banker in a suit reading it with eyebrows raised while a founder sits on a yoga ball.

## 2. "Don't be evil" (motto)
- Year: coined ~1999-2001; in IPO letter 2004; Alphabet adopted "Do the right thing" 2015; removed from Google code of conduct preface April 2018 (kept in final sentence).
- Description: Informal motto attributed to engineers Paul Buchheit and/or Amit Patel at an early corporate-values meeting (Buchheit says "early 2000"; another account credits Patel in 1999). The IPO letter included a section headed "Don't be evil," saying the company would forgo some short-term gains to do good. In 2018 it was moved from the code of conduct's preface to its last sentence.
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Don't_be_evil ; https://quoteinvestigator.com/2018/11/07/no-evil/ ; https://www.snopes.com/fact-check/google-motto-dont-be-evil/
- Visual: a whiteboard list of corporate values where someone keeps dragging "Don't be evil" back to the top with a marker; later, the same phrase on a code-of-conduct document being scrolled down to the very bottom.

## 3. 20% time
- Year: in place by early 2000s; formally described in 2004 IPO letter; widely reported as "as good as dead" in 2013 (Quartz report).
- Description: The IPO letter said engineers were encouraged to spend 20% of their time "on what they think will most benefit Google" (Page & Brin, 2004). In practice many ex-Googlers described it as "120% time" (done on top of regular work), and by 2013 reports said managers required approval and it was rarely used. Marissa Mayer (ex-Google, then Yahoo CEO) told a 2013 Yahoo all-hands the "dirty little secret" was that "It's really 120% time." [VERIFIED via secondary reports]
- Source: https://www.irishtimes.com/business/technology/innovation-or-overtime-mystery-surrounds-google-s-20-time-1.3027793 ; https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=6223466 (thread on Quartz 2013 "as good as dead" article) ; https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/marissa-mayer-reveals-dirty-little-secret-googles-20-time-carlson
- Visual: a weekly calendar with Friday shaded "20%"; the shaded block then gets pushed onto Saturday, then onto 11pm.

## 4. Products attributed to 20% time
- Year: Google News (2002), Google Suggest (2004), Orkut (2004), AdSense for content (2003), Gmail (2004), Google Talk, Google Sky; later Google Cardboard (2014).
- Description: The 2004 IPO letter itself names AdSense for content and Google News as prototyped in 20% time, plus Google Suggest and Orkut. Gmail is often attributed to 20% time but Paul Buchheit's work on it is also described as part of his regular role — attribution is contested. Google Cardboard (2014) is one of the last widely cited 20% products.
- Source: https://www.thestreet.com/opinion/text-of-googles-letter-to-prospective-shareholders-10157519 ; https://www.belgraviaadvisers.com/post/googles-20-time ; https://www.phonearena.com/news/Googles-famous-20-time-which-created-Gmail-GTalk-and-Google-Sky-may-be-dead_id46492
- Visual: a garage-style workbench where an engineer assembles a cardboard VR viewer out of a pizza box; a trophy shelf labeled with product icons (envelope, newspaper, telescope).

## 5. TGIF all-hands (and Dory Q&A)
- Year: started 1999 (under 60 employees); weekly through 2019; later moved to Thursday ("TGI Almost Friday") for Asia/Europe time zones; cut to monthly business-focused meetings in Nov 2019 after leaks; Dory replaced by AI "Ask" tool in 2024.
- Description: Weekly all-hands where Larry and Sergey shared what they were working on, welcomed new hires, and took open, direct questions from any employee. Questions were submitted and upvoted on an internal tool called Dory. A 1999 video of an early TGIF circulates on YouTube; early meetings reportedly included birthdays and silly string.
- Source: https://www.cnbc.com/2019/11/15/google-cancels-tgif-weekly-all-hands-meetings.html ; https://www.seroundtable.com/google-tgif-13338.html ; https://chaesangjung.medium.com/the-most-primitive-form-of-communication-9b68c4c2b132 ; https://www.hrgrapevine.com/us/content/article/2024-08-30-google-pioneers-ai-leadership-meetings
- Visual: two founders on a small stage with a microphone stand; a giant screen showing a question list with up-vote arrows; the top question has 4,000 votes and the founders slowly look at each other.

## 8. Noogler propeller hat
- Year: tradition dating to early company (idea credited to Doug Edwards, employee #59, ~1999-2000); still in use in 2010s; featured in film "The Internship" (2013)
- Description: New employees ("Nooglers") receive a beanie in Google's blue/red/yellow/green colors with a plastic propeller on top, traditionally worn at their first TGIF so veterans can spot and welcome them. One specimen is in the Computer History Museum's "Revolution" collection.

## 9. Testing on the Toilet (TotT)
- Year: first episode April 2006; public blog version from 2007; still running.
- Description: One-page testing tips posted inside bathroom stalls across Google, born in a Testing Grouplet brainstorm in early 2006 that started as a joke. Episodes covered topics such as dependency injection and code coverage, eventually reaching roughly 500 stalls worldwide. Reception was polarized; some objected to the invasion of personal space.
- Source: https://mike-bland.com/2011/10/25/testing-on-the-toilet.html ; https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch11.html ; https://testing.googleblog.com/
- Visual: a toilet stall door with a laminated one-page flyer titled "Episode 42: Mock Objects", taped at eye level; a pair of feet visible under the door.

## 10. Readability certification
- Year: began informally in early days (Craig Silverstein, employee #3, reviewing new hires' code line by line); formalized as company grew (mid-2000s). Documented in "Software Engineering at Google" (O'Reilly, 2020).
- Description: Every changelist needs approval from someone holding "readability" in that language (e.g., C++, Java, Python, Go). Engineers earn it by submitting code to a pool of readability reviewers until certified. The book reports about 1-2% of engineers are readability reviewers and around 20% are in the process at any time.
- Source: https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch03.html ; https://read.engineerscodex.com/p/how-google-writes-clean-maintainable ; https://dev.to/pullrequest/getting-the-certification-to-review-code-at-google-55ng
- Visual: a framed diploma reading "Certified Readable: Python" hung over a desk; a ticket-dispenser queue of engineers waiting for a stamp.

## 11. Mondrian -> Critique (code review), and LGTM
- Year: Mondrian demoed Nov 2006 by Guido van Rossum (joined Google 2005); open-source clone Rietveld released 2008; Gerrit forked from Rietveld (2008-09); Critique later replaced Mondrian internally (early 2010s) [date UNVERIFIED].
- Description: Python creator Guido van Rossum built Mondrian, a web-based side-by-side diff and comment tool, used by nearly every Google engineer daily on top of Perforce. Its successor Critique is the current internal tool. Reviewers approve changes with "LGTM" ("looks good to me"), which became industry shorthand.
- Source: https://lwn.net/Articles/281031/ ; https://developers.googleblog.com/guido-van-rossum-releases-mondrian-clone-rietveld/ ; https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch19.html
- Visual: a split-screen diff with red and green lines; a comment bubble that simply says "LGTM" stamped like a rubber stamp.

## 12. The monorepo (Perforce -> Piper)
- Year: single Perforce repo from early days; Piper (in-house VCS) by ~2012; stats as of January 2015; CACM paper July 2016 (Potvin & Levenberg).
- Description: Almost all of Google's code lives in one repository with trunk-based development. The paper's January 2015 snapshot: about 1 billion files, 2 billion lines of code, 9 million source files, 35 million commits, 86 TB, used by 25,000+ engineers. Piper was built because Perforce no longer scaled.
- Source: https://cacm.acm.org/research/why-google-stores-billions-of-lines-of-code-in-a-single-repository/ ; https://graphite.com/blog/google-perforce-to-piper-migration
- Visual: a single enormous tree trunk with thousands of tiny engineers climbing it, each on their own branch twig that all reconnect to the trunk.

## 13. Blaze / Bazel (build system)
- Year: Blaze internal since mid-2000s [start date UNVERIFIED]; open-sourced as Bazel (an anagram of Blaze) March 2015; beta Sept 2015.
- Description: Blaze builds essentially everything at Google, from tiny experiments up to Search, emphasizing fast, correct, reproducible builds in the monorepo. The open-source port was named Bazel by anagram.
- Source: https://jmmv.dev/2015/04/on-bazel-and-open-source.html ; https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch18.html ; https://www.infoq.com/news/2015/09/bazel
- Visual: Scrabble tiles spelling B-L-A-Z-E being rearranged into B-A-Z-E-L.

## 14. Borg (cluster manager)
- Year: internal since ~2003-2004; publicly described in EuroSys 2015 paper (Verma, Pedrosa, Korupolu, Oppenheimer, Tune, Wilkes); Kubernetes (2014) is its open-source descendant.
- Description: Borg runs Google's services and batch jobs across "cells" of thousands to tens of thousands of machines, with a Paxos-replicated Borgmaster and Borglet agents on each machine. Named after Star Trek's collective; Kubernetes was its successor, built by many former Borg engineers.
- Source: https://research.google/pubs/large-scale-cluster-management-at-google-with-borg/ ; https://kubernetes.io/blog/2015/04/borg-predecessor-to-kubernetes/ ; https://queue.acm.org/detail.cfm?id=2898444
- Visual: a giant cube-shaped server hall with rows of identical machines; a tiny job icon being placed onto one rack by a robotic hand.

## 15. Code Search (internal) and public Google Code Search
- Year: public Google Code Search launched Oct 5, 2006, shut down Jan 2012; internal Code Search continues (described in "Software Engineering at Google," ch. 17).
- Description: Started as an intern project after Jeff Dean suggested a "web grep" over public source code, using a trigram index for regex search. When it was shut down, Russ Cox published "Regular Expression Matching with a Trigram Index (or: How Google Code Search Worked)" and open-sourced a small version. Internally, engineers search the whole monorepo from a browser.
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Code_Search ; https://swtch.com/~rsc/regexp/regexp4.html ; https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch17.html
- Visual: a magnifying glass sliding over an endless scroll of code; three-letter tiles ("fun", "unc", "nct") lighting up.

## 16. Design docs culture
- Year: long-standing practice; best-known public description is Malte Ubl's "Design Docs at Google" (July 6, 2020).
- Description: Before coding a significant system, authors write a relatively informal document covering context, goals and non-goals, the design, alternatives considered, and trade-offs, then circulate it for comments. Ubl describes them as informal so the "cost of change" stays low. [Common satirical angle: docs written then never updated — opinion, not fact.]
- Source: https://www.industrialempathy.com/posts/design-docs-at-google/ ; https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=23915521
- Visual: a document with a "Goals / Non-Goals" table, its margins crowded with dozens of colored comment bubbles, each spawning more replies.

## 17. Site Reliability Engineering (SRE) and the SRE book
- Year: SRE team founded 2003 by Ben Treynor Sloss (started with seven engineers); book "Site Reliability Engineering: How Google Runs Production Systems" published April 2016 by O'Reilly (eds. Beyer, Jones, Petoff, Murphy), free online.
- Description: Treynor Sloss's definition: SRE is "what happens when you ask a software engineer to design an operations team." Key ideas: SLOs with error budgets that pause launches when exhausted, a cap of 50% of SRE time on operational "toil," and blameless postmortems.
- Source: https://sre.google/sre-book/introduction/ ; https://research.google/pubs/site-reliability-engineering-how-google-runs-production-systems/ ; https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/devops-sre/the-sre-book-turns-6
- Visual: a pager on a nightstand glowing at 3:07 a.m.; a fuel-gauge style "error budget" meter dipping into red.

## 23. Brain-teaser interview era (and its renunciation)
- Year: early-mid 2000s practice; renounced June 2013 (Laszlo Bock to NYT's Adam Bryant).
- Description: Google became famous for puzzle questions (e.g., how many golf balls fit in an airplane). In 2013 SVP of People Operations Laszlo Bock said brainteasers "are a complete waste of time" and "serve primarily to make the interviewer feel smart." Google also found GPAs and test scores did not predict performance beyond the first couple of years after college.
- Source: https://www.nytimes.com/2013/06/20/business/in-head-hunting-big-data-may-not-be-such-a-big-deal.html (Adam Bryant, NYT, June 19-20, 2013) ; https://thenextweb.com/news/google-reveals-that-its-seemingly-impossible-interview-brainteasers-are-a-complete-waste-of-time

## 24. Engineers-first hiring: transcripts, hiring committees, Larry approves every hire

Chris DiBona recounts Larry pausing non-engineering hiring until engineering was back in the majority. [DiBona anecdote from his personal Substack; treat as first-person recollection.]

## 30. Death benefits
- Year: formalized 2011; revealed August 2012 (Laszlo Bock interview, Forbes).

## 33. The 2001 "no managers" experiment / Project Oxygen
- Year: July 2001 (Larry Page removed engineering managers; engineers reported to VP Wayne Rosing); Project Oxygen research 2008-2009, reported by NYT March 2011 ("Google's Quest to Build a Better Boss"). [FROM MEMORY, NOT RE-VERIFIED]
- Description: In 2001 Page, distrustful of middle management, eliminated the layer of engineering managers so that over 100 engineers reported to one executive; the experiment was reversed as it proved unworkable (recounted in Steven Levy's "In the Plex," 2011, and Douglas Edwards' "I'm Feeling Lucky"). Later, People Operations' Project Oxygen set out to test whether managers mattered and concluded they did, producing a list of eight good-manager behaviors, with technical expertise ranked last.

## 34. Associate Product Manager (APM) program and the engineer:PM ratio
